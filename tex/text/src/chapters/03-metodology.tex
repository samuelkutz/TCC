\chapter{METHODOLOGY}
\label{chap:methodology}

The theory of Chapter~2 becomes a set of runnable experiments here, and this chapter describes how they were built and read. Its layout follows the life of a single experiment. Everything downstream rests on three shared foundations, so we lay those first: the computing environment and the reproducibility rules that let any result be regenerated (Section~\ref{sec:m_setup}), the pseudospectral data that serve at once as training targets and as ground truth (Section~\ref{sec:m_dataset}), and the error metrics that score every model on the same scale (Section~\ref{sec:m_metrics}). With the foundations in place, Section~\ref{sec:m_ladder} presents the six models as a deliberate progression, each rung adding one ingredient to the one below it, from a plain data regressor up to a physics-constrained operator over the whole family. Section~\ref{sec:m_probe} then turns to the one behaviour every rung shares, spectral bias, and sets up two probes of its mechanism. Section~\ref{sec:m_config} collects every hyperparameter into a single table.

% ===============================================================
\section{Setup, Solver, and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:m_setup}

Every piece of software behind these experiments was written for this work rather than taken from an existing package. The code runs on Python~3.11 with PyTorch~2.12 and CUDA~13.0 \parencite{paszke2019pytorch}, and all timings come from one NVIDIA GeForce GTX~1660~SUPER card with $6$\,GB of memory. NumPy handles array bookkeeping outside the training loops, and Matplotlib and Plotly draw the figures. We used no pre-trained weights, no third-party operator-learning library and no external dataset. The pseudospectral solver, the multilayer perceptron, the Fourier layers and each optimization loop are our own, which is what let us instrument them for the spectral diagnostics that follow in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}. The complete implementation is available at \url{https://github.com/samuelkutz/TCC}.

Each learned architecture tracks its original reference as closely as we could manage. The Fourier Neural Operator follows \textcite{li2020fourier, li2021}, the Physics-Informed Neural Operator follows \textcite{li2023pino} and its released code \parencite{li2023pino_code}, and the Physics-Informed Neural Network follows \textcite{raissi2019}. Every departure from a reference is flagged at the point where it is made.

Two conventions make the results reproducible. A single global seed of $37$ initializes the Python, NumPy and PyTorch generators at the start of each run, and the cuDNN backend runs in deterministic mode with autotuning disabled. The spatial and temporal domains stay fixed throughout at
\begin{equation}\label{eq:domain}
    x \in [-L, L], \quad L = 60, \qquad t \in [0, T], \quad T = 30,
\end{equation}
and every experiment shares the initial condition of Equation~\eqref{eq:boussinesq} at unit amplitude $A = 1$, so that $\eta(x,0) = \sech^2(x)$ and $u(x,0) = 0$. A unit-height, unit-width crest sits at the centre of a domain $120$ units across, wide enough that the two counter-propagating pulses of the wave-equation limit~\eqref{eq:dalembert_eta}--\eqref{eq:dalembert_u} never reach the periodic boundary within the simulated horizon. Table~\ref{tab:sim_params} collects the simulation, dataset and evaluation constants that hold everywhere; each is restated below at the point where it first matters.

\section{Building the Parametric Dataset}
\label{sec:m_dataset}

The reference solutions carry two jobs at once. They are the supervised targets the data-driven regimes learn from, and they are the ground truth every model is later scored against. The pseudospectral Runge--Kutta solver of Section~\ref{sec:pseudospectral} produces both, and its spectral spatial accuracy is what a dispersive wave problem needs from a high-fidelity baseline \parencite{engsigkarup2012, trefethen2000}.

The parameter family is one-dimensional by design. Following Section~\ref{sec:dataset}, we hold the initial condition fixed and vary only the PDE coefficients, and we tie them together as $\alpha = \beta$ so that the two-parameter Boussinesq family collapses to a single axis of difficulty. Near the small end of that axis the dynamics stay close to linear and the solution energy occupies a handful of low modes; near the large end, steepening and dispersion combine to sharpen the crests and to spread their energy across a wider band of wavenumbers. We sample the axis uniformly at $M = 20$ points,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:param_grid}
    \alpha_i = \beta_i \in \operatorname{linspace}(0.1,\, 4.0,\, 20), \qquad i = 1, \ldots, 20,
\end{equation}
running from the near-linear value $0.1$ up to the strongly nonlinear value $4.0$.

Each parameter in~\eqref{eq:param_grid} is fed to the solver on $N_x = 128$ spatial points and marched through $N_t = 128$ time levels, that is $127$ Runge--Kutta steps over $[0,T]$, with spacings
\begin{equation}\label{eq:spacings}
    \Delta x = \frac{2L}{N_x} = \frac{120}{128} = 0.9375,
    \qquad
    \Delta t = \frac{T}{N_t - 1} = \frac{30}{127} \approx 0.2362.
\end{equation}
A solve returns the two space-time fields $\eta_i(x_j, t_n)$ and $u_i(x_j, t_n)$ on the $128 \times 128$ grid. We store them in the channel-first tensors the two-dimensional operators expect, four channels in and two out:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tensor_shapes}
    X \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times 4 \times N_x \times N_t},
    \qquad
    Y \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times 2 \times N_x \times N_t}.
\end{equation}
The four input channels hold, at every grid point, the initial elevation $\eta_i(x_j, 0)$, the initial velocity $u_i(x_j, 0)$ and the two constant coefficient fields $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$, with the initial-condition channels broadcast along the time axis so that the operator receives a full space-time array. The two output channels hold the reference fields $\eta_i$ and $u_i$. From here on every panel reports the surface elevation $\eta$ alone, since it carries the wave structure of interest and the velocity field behaves the same way.

These channels mix quantities of unlike magnitude: an elevation of order one, a velocity field, and coefficients that span more than an order of magnitude. To place them on a common footing we map every tensor into the unit interval with one affine, channel-wise min--max transform computed once over the whole dataset,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:minmax}
    \tilde v = \frac{v - v_{\min}}{v_{\max} - v_{\min} + \varepsilon},
    \qquad \varepsilon = 10^{-12},
\end{equation}
where $v_{\min}$ and $v_{\max}$ are taken per channel across the sample, spatial and temporal axes. Operators train and predict in this normalized space, and their outputs return to physical units by inverting~\eqref{eq:minmax} before any metric or residual is formed. One consequence shapes how the physics-only regimes of Section~\ref{sec:m_pino} must be read: because $v_{\min}$ and $v_{\max}$ come from the reference solutions, even a model trained without a data-fitting term still inherits the output scale of the dataset through~\eqref{eq:minmax}. ``No data'' therefore means no supervised residual on the interior fields, and it stops short of full independence from the reference.

\section{A Common Yardstick}
\label{sec:m_metrics}

A fair comparison needs one ruler that every model is held against, so that a measured difference reflects the architecture and not the measurement. Every method is scored against a freshly recomputed pseudospectral reference with the shared metrics defined below. Three parameter values sample the difficulty axis,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eval_params}
    \alpha = \beta \in \{0.1,\; 3.21,\; 4.2\},
\end{equation}
one near-linear, one intermediate and one strongly nonlinear. The middle value $3.21$ is our default whenever a single representative parameter is called for, and it is where the single-parameter PINNs of Section~\ref{sec:m_pinn} are trained. To probe the discretization-invariance operators are meant to enjoy, each trained operator is also queried, with no retraining, on grids of resolution
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eval_res}
    N \in \{128,\; 256,\; 512\},
\end{equation}
the training resolution together with two and four times finer. For each such query we resample the input channels of Section~\ref{sec:m_dataset} onto the finer grid and recompute the reference at the matching resolution.

Let $\eta_{\text{true}}$ and $\eta_{\text{pred}}$ be the reference and predicted elevation on a common space-time grid. We report three complementary quantities.

\paragraph{Time-resolved relative error.} At each time level $t_n$ we form the spatial $L^2$ discrepancy and divide it by the norm of the reference at that instant,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel_time}
    \mathcal{E}(t_n) = \frac{\bigl\| \eta_{\text{pred}}(\cdot, t_n) - \eta_{\text{true}}(\cdot, t_n) \bigr\|_2}{\bigl\| \eta_{\text{true}}(\cdot, t_n) \bigr\|_2}.
\end{equation}
Read against time, $\mathcal{E}(t_n)$ locates where along the trajectory the error builds up, and its spread over all time levels is summarized by a box plot. This is the quantity behind the parameter and resolution panels of Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\paragraph{Spectral relative error.} To see \emph{which} spatial frequencies are wrong, we take the spatial real DFT at a fixed time and compare amplitudes mode by mode. Writing $\hat\eta(k, t_n)$ for the amplitude at wavenumber index $k$, the per-mode relative error is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rel_spec}
    \mathcal{E}_{\text{spec}}(k, t_n) = \frac{\bigl| \, |\hat\eta_{\text{pred}}(k,t_n)| - |\hat\eta_{\text{true}}(k,t_n)| \, \bigr|}{\max\!\bigl(|\hat\eta_{\text{true}}(k,t_n)|,\, \delta\bigr)},
\end{equation}
where the floor $\delta$, set proportional to the peak amplitude, keeps the ratio finite at modes whose reference amplitude is near zero. Averaging over the retained modes yields one spectral number per time level, and the spectral panels display both the mode-resolved curves at $t = 0$, $t = T/2$ and $t = T$ and the time evolution of the average.

\paragraph{Frequency-band error during training.} To watch spectral bias form as the optimizer runs, we split the spatial spectrum of the error into three contiguous wavenumber bands and log each band error every $500$ iterations. The tracked ratio differs slightly from~\eqref{eq:rel_spec}: here we apply the spatial real DFT to the \emph{error field} $\eta_{\text{pred}} - \eta_{\text{true}}$ and to the reference, average each amplitude over the time levels, and take the quotient,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:band_err}
    \mathcal{E}_{\text{band}}(k) = \frac{\big\langle\, \bigl|\widehat{(\eta_{\text{pred}} - \eta_{\text{true}})}(k, t_n)\bigr| \,\big\rangle_{t_n}}{\big\langle\, |\hat\eta_{\text{true}}(k, t_n)| \,\big\rangle_{t_n} + \varepsilon},
    \qquad \varepsilon = 10^{-12}.
\end{equation}
Transforming the error before taking magnitudes makes this metric sensitive to the phase of the error, rather than to a mode-by-mode magnitude comparison. With $N_k = \lfloor N_x/2 \rfloor + 1$ the number of retained real-DFT modes, the low, mid and high bands cut the index range into quarters,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bands}
    k < \tfrac{N_k}{4}, \qquad \tfrac{N_k}{4} \le k < \tfrac{N_k}{2}, \qquad k \ge \tfrac{N_k}{2},
\end{equation}
which fall near $N_x/8$ and $N_x/4$ on the full grid. Each band error is the average of~\eqref{eq:band_err} over the modes in that band. These curves feed the band-tracking comparison of Chapter~\ref{chap:results}. One asymmetry in the logging matters for how the curves are read: each operator is tracked on the first dataset sample, $\alpha = \beta = 0.1$, while the single-parameter MLP and PINN are tracked on their own training solution at $\alpha = \beta = 3.21$. We keep this mismatch in reference sample in mind when reading the results.

\section{A Ladder of Approximators}
\label{sec:m_ladder}

The six models form a ladder rather than a list. Each rung keeps the machinery of the rung below and adds exactly one ingredient, so that any change in behaviour can be traced to that single addition. The first rung is a plain regressor that knows only the data. The second wraps the same network in the governing equation. The third lifts the problem from one solution to the whole family. The fourth adds the equation to that family-wide operator. Climbing the ladder in this order isolates, one step at a time, what physics contributes, what operator learning contributes, and what the two contribute together.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The Plain Regressor: MLP}
\label{sec:m_mlp}

The bottom rung is a Multilayer Perceptron that fits one solution from data alone. It is the fully connected network of Section~\ref{subsec:mlp}, sending a space-time point to an elevation, $(x,t) \mapsto \eta_\theta(x,t)$, with two input units, one output unit, four hidden layers of $256$ neurons and hyperbolic-tangent activations, for $198{,}401$ trainable parameters. This same backbone is what the PINN of the next rung reuses, so any difference between them will come from the training signal and not the architecture.

Training is pure supervised regression. The objective is the mean-squared error~\eqref{eq:data_loss_nn} between the network output and the pseudospectral reference at the fixed parameter $\alpha = \beta = 3.21$, on the training grid of resolution $128$. We optimize with Adam \parencite{kingma2014adam} at learning rate $10^{-3}$ for $15{,}000$ iterations, taking one full-batch gradient step over the entire reference field per iteration. This matches the PINN budget exactly.

The value of this rung lies in what its objective omits. It carries no PDE-residual term and no initial-condition term, so the equation~\eqref{eq:boussinesq} never enters and the network sees only data. That makes it the cleanest testbed for spectral bias in this work: with nothing competing against the data fit, the order in which the network resolves the spatial frequencies of the target is set by the fitting dynamics alone. Any low-first, high-last ordering it shows is then spectral bias free of confounders. We measure that ordering with the frequency-band error~\eqref{eq:band_err}--\eqref{eq:bands}, tracked on the network's own training solution at $\alpha = \beta = 3.21$, and we tie it to the eigenvalue mechanism through the diagnostics of Section~\ref{sec:m_probe}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Adding the Equation: PINN}
\label{sec:m_pinn}

The second rung keeps the feed-forward backbone and adds the Boussinesq residual to its loss. The PINN of Section~\ref{subsec:pinn} carries the solution as one continuous map $(x,t) \mapsto (\eta_\theta, u_\theta)$ and is therefore tied to a single parameter pair. Each network is fully connected with two input units, two output units, four hidden layers of $256$ neurons and hyperbolic-tangent activations, for $198{,}658$ trainable parameters, and it is optimized with Adam \parencite{kingma2014adam} at learning rate $10^{-3}$ for $15{,}000$ iterations.

The composite objective~\eqref{eq:pinn_loss_boussinesq} has three parts. The residual term is evaluated at $5{,}000$ interior collocation points resampled uniformly at random over $[-L,L] \times [0,T]$ at every iteration, and the derivatives entering the Boussinesq operators $\mathcal{D}_1, \mathcal{D}_2$ come from automatic differentiation, so they carry no discretization error. The initial-condition term is enforced at $500$ equispaced points at $t = 0$. The data term, when present, is a supervised mean-squared error against a subsample of the pseudospectral reference. That last term is the one ingredient we toggle, giving two regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PINN (no data)}: purely physics-informed, data weight $0$; the network sees only the equation and the initial condition.
    \item \textbf{PINN (with data)}: hybrid, data weight $1$; the network additionally fits the reference at the training parameter.
\end{itemize}
Both regimes train at $\alpha = \beta = 3.21$, the intermediate difficulty of~\eqref{eq:eval_params}, chosen in the nonlinear range where spectral bias should be most visible \parencite{wang2024, zheng2023}. Setting the two side by side isolates the effect of the physics term with the architecture and the budget held fixed.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Learning the Operator: FNO}
\label{sec:m_fno}

The third rung changes the target of learning. Instead of one solution, the Fourier Neural Operator learns the map from parameter to solution for the entire family~\eqref{eq:param_grid} in a single training, so a new $\alpha = \beta$ then costs one forward pass rather than a fresh optimization. Its architecture is the general operator of Section~\ref{subsec:fno}: a pointwise lifting takes the six input features at each grid point, the four channels of~\eqref{eq:tensor_shapes} together with the two normalized grid coordinates, up to a channel width of $32$; four Fourier layers each combine a spectral convolution truncated to $16$ modes per axis with a local $1\times 1$ convolution and a GELU activation; and a pointwise projection returns the two output channels. This gives $2{,}106{,}146$ trainable parameters, an order of magnitude above the pointwise models. Training minimizes the supervised mean-squared error~\eqref{eq:fno_loss} on the normalized fields with Adam at learning rate $10^{-3}$ for $15{,}000$ iterations; with only $M = 20$ samples, each iteration is effectively a full-batch step.

One implementation choice is worth pausing on, because it drives a result in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}. Each Fourier layer applies a two-dimensional real DFT over the last two axes of the feature array, which are the spatial \emph{and the temporal} axes. The operator thus treats time as a second periodic spectral dimension and emits the whole space-time solution in one pass. That keeps the architecture uniform, but it revives the periodicity assumption of Section~\ref{subsubsec:fno_time}: the Boussinesq solution is not periodic in time, so the implicit wrap-around at $t = T$ excites the Gibbs phenomenon \parencite{gibbs1899}. We record the resulting boundary artifact as a measurable behaviour, and it later separates the FNO from its physics-informed successor.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Physics on the Operator: PINO}
\label{sec:m_pino}

The top rung adds the equation to the operator, completing the ladder by combining the two previous additions. The Physics-Informed Neural Operator keeps the FNO backbone of Section~\ref{sec:m_fno} untouched and changes only the objective, appending the Boussinesq residual~\eqref{eq:pino_loss} to the data loss. Evaluating that residual means differentiating the predicted space-time array, and, following the reference implementation \parencite{li2023pino_code}, we treat the two directions differently.

Spatial derivatives are taken pseudospectrally with the wavenumbers $\xi_k$ of~\eqref{eq:xi_def}: we multiply the spatial DFT of the predicted field by $i\xi_k$ for the first derivative and by $-\xi_k^2$ for the second, then transform back. Temporal derivatives are taken by finite differences on the grid, second-order central in the interior and first-order one-sided at the two temporal boundaries. The mixed term $u_{xxt}$ is formed by taking the spatial second derivative spectrally and then applying the temporal stencil. The residual is assembled in \emph{physical} units: the normalized output and coefficients are mapped back through the inverse of~\eqref{eq:minmax} before the derivatives are formed, so the constraint enforces the true Boussinesq balance rather than a rescaled surrogate.

One further detail sets the residual apart from the data term. The supervised loss is evaluated on the native $128 \times 128$ training grid, but the physics residual is evaluated on a finer $256 \times 256$ grid, onto which the predicted field is spectrally interpolated before the derivatives are formed. A denser residual grid places a stricter, better-resolved constraint on the high-wavenumber content, at the price of extra transforms per iteration, and it means the physics term already sees resolution $256$ during training, a fact we return to when reading the zero-shot resolution behaviour in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}. The objective is the weighted sum
\begin{equation}\label{eq:met_pino_loss}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{PINO}} = \lambda_{\text{pde}}\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{pde}} + \lambda_{\text{ic}}\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{ic}} + \lambda_{\text{data}}\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}},
\end{equation}
with the interior residual, the initial-condition term and the supervised term of~\eqref{eq:pino_loss}. We weight the physics residual well above the other two, $\lambda_{\text{pde}} = 100$ against $\lambda_{\text{ic}} = 1$, so that the equation constraint leads the optimization; this emphasis was needed for the residual to leave a visible mark against the operator's strong pull toward the data. As with the PINN, the data weight is the single toggle:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PINO (with data)}: $\lambda_{\text{pde}} = 100$, $\lambda_{\text{ic}} = \lambda_{\text{data}} = 1$; physics and data act together, the residual dominating the objective and the data term anchoring the solution to the reference.
    \item \textbf{PINO (no data)}: $\lambda_{\text{pde}} = 100$, $\lambda_{\text{ic}} = 1$, $\lambda_{\text{data}} = 0$; the operator is guided by the equation and the initial condition only, subject to the normalization caveat of Section~\ref{sec:m_dataset}.
\end{itemize}
Optimization is again Adam at learning rate $10^{-3}$ for $15{,}000$ iterations, and every remaining hyperparameter matches the FNO, so any change in behaviour traces back to the physics term alone.

\section{Probing the Spectral Bias}
\label{sec:m_probe}

Each rung of the ladder shares one behaviour, the spectral bias derived in Section~\ref{subsec:spectral_bias}, and its cause deserves a direct examination rather than an inference from the training curves. We examine it with two probes of the same mechanism. The first looks at the Neural Tangent Kernel of a real Boussinesq PINN and asks whether the theory's assumptions hold there. The second builds a controlled model in which those assumptions hold exactly, so that the theory's quantitative predictions can be checked with nothing approximated. Together they connect the abstract eigenvalue analysis to the behaviour measured on the full architectures.

\subsection{The Kernel of a Real PINN}
\label{sec:m_probe_ntk}

The first probe inspects the Neural Tangent Kernel of the PINN residual directly. We train three networks of increasing width, $N_{\mathrm{MLP}} \in \{8, 128, 512\}$ with four hidden layers, at $\alpha = \beta = 3.21$ on the physics-only objective, using stochastic gradient descent with a small learning rate ($10^{-5}$), the regime in which the frozen-kernel approximation~\eqref{eq:error_linear_ode} is expected to hold. The empirical kernel $K = J J^{\mathsf{T}}$ is built explicitly at a fixed set of $40$ probe points: automatic differentiation supplies the parameter gradient of each Boussinesq residual, and their Gram matrix~\eqref{eq:ntk_entry} is the kernel. Every $500$ iterations we log three quantities, the relative parameter drift $\|\theta - \theta_0\| / \|\theta_0\|$, the relative kernel drift $\|K - K_0\| / \|K_0\|$, and the eigenvalue spectrum of $K$ at initialization and at the end of training. The first two ask whether the kernel really stays put, as lazy training assumes; the third exposes the eigenvalue disparity that~\eqref{eq:training_time} names as the engine of spectral bias.

\subsection{A Toy Model Where the Theory Is Exact}
\label{sec:m_probe_theory}

The kernel probe can only check whether the theory's assumptions nearly hold, because a real network freezes its kernel only approximately. The second probe removes that gap. We build a small model in which the frozen-kernel dynamics of Section~\ref{subsec:spectral_bias} hold exactly, so that the predicted error decay, spectral decay and per-mode iteration counts can be compared against measurement with nothing left to interpretation.

The construction is a Fourier-feature model, linear in its trainable parameters, fit to a periodic target on a regular grid. We sample a periodic target on $N$ equispaced points and map each point through a fixed bank of Fourier features, sines and cosines at a range of wavenumbers; the only trainable parameters are the weights of a single linear layer on top of those features. Because the output is linear in those weights, the error Jacobian $J$ does not depend on the parameters at all: it equals its initial value $J_0$ for the whole run, and the empirical kernel $K_0 = J_0 J_0^{\mathsf{T}}$ is genuinely constant rather than slowly drifting. Lazy training is then a property of the model by construction, not an approximation.

We train the model by full-batch gradient descent on the mean-squared error, so a single step is exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:theory_step}
    \theta \leftarrow \theta - \mu\, J_0^{\mathsf{T}} e,
\end{equation}
the linearized update~\eqref{eq:error_discrete_step} with no higher-order correction. We set the learning rate to $\mu = 1/\lambda_{\max}$, with $\lambda_{\max}$ the largest eigenvalue of $K_0$, which sits safely inside the stability bound $\mu < 2/\lambda_{\max}$ of Section~\ref{subsec:spectral_bias}, so every eigendirection contracts monotonically. Under these choices the error obeys $e^{(n)} = (I - \mu K_0)^n e_0$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:error_discrete} exactly, and the three predictions of the theory become testable one at a time:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Error decay $e(\tau)$.} We project the measured error onto each eigenvector $v_k$ of $K_0$ and compare the amplitude against the predicted $(1 - \mu\lambda_k)^n$ of~\eqref{eq:error_discrete}, following each eigendirection down to the float64 floor.
    \item \textbf{Spectral decay $\hat e(\tau)$.} We take the discrete Fourier transform of the error and compare the measured per-mode amplitude against the predicted decay of~\eqref{eq:fourier_evolution}, checking that low wavenumbers resolve first and high wavenumbers last.
    \item \textbf{Per-mode iteration count $n_k$.} For each mode we record the iteration at which its amplitude first drops below a target precision $\varepsilon$ and compare it against the closed-form estimate $n_k = \lceil \ln(|c_k(0)|/\varepsilon)/(\mu\lambda_k)\rceil$ of~\eqref{eq:iteration_count}.
\end{itemize}
These predictions open the results of Chapter~\ref{chap:results}, where they anchor the reading of the spectral-bias curves measured on the full models.
