\chapter{NUMERICAL RESULTS}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter reports what the experiments of Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} produced. Section~\ref{sec:r_cost} accounts for what each method costs to train. The next three sections then take the methods along one axis at a time with Section~\ref{sec:r_params} sweeping through model parameters at a fixed resolution, Section~\ref{sec:r_res} holding the parameter fixed and querying the operators off their training grid to check zero-shot superresolution, and Section~\ref{sec:r_spectrum} fixing both and comparing the spectra mode by mode at three time instants. The temporal artifact given by the Gibbs phenomenon that the physics term corrects is isolated in Section~\ref{sec:r_gibbs}. Section~\ref{sec:r_bias} aims to establish spectral bias with the initial-kernel analysis of a minimal MLP fitting the Boussinesq wave profile, and Section~\ref{sec:r_bands} follows the error through the frequency bands as training proceeds, showing the phenomenon exists for all methods. Section~\ref{sec:r_synthesis} gathers all of our findings. The pseudospectral solver is the ground truth throughout.

\section{Training Cost}
\label{sec:r_cost}

Before asking how well the methods perform, we ask what they cost to train. Table~\ref{tab:training} lists the size, wall-clock training time, and final training loss of the six trained methods. It is important to notice that neural operator methods carry many more trainable parameters than the neural network methods. Specifically, PINO is slower because it assembles its physics residual on the finer $256 \times 256$ grid (Table~\ref{tab:m_config}) at every iteration.

The panels agree only that the error is non-monotone in the parameter; which end is worst depends on the method. They differ in that the FNO curves end in a sharp terminal spike and the PINO curves do not. Section~\ref{sec:r_gibbs} takes that up.

Discretization invariance holds in the geometric sense. At every resolution the operator returns a smooth, coherent surface with the correct wave structure, with no retraining and no architectural change, since the input is simply resampled on the finer grid.

Queried below that resolution the operator loses the residual-resolved detail; queried above it, it meets high-wavenumber content it never learned to produce, since its spectral layers keep only a fixed number of Fourier modes per axis. The plain FNO, whose only constraint is the data at its training resolution, degrades monotonically over the same sweep, best on the grid it was trained on and worse on every finer one (Figure~\ref{fig:res_fno}).

In every panel the black curve is the reference spatial spectrum and the dashed orange curve is the prediction, with the per-mode absolute error beside each spectrum and the mode-averaged error against time at the foot of each figure. One reading note applies throughout. The per-mode error~\eqref{eq:m_abs_spec} is a difference of amplitudes in the units of $\eta$, not a ratio, so it is read against the reference amplitude at the same wavenumber.

\section{Temporal Stability from the Physics Residual}
\label{sec:r_gibbs}

The temporal growth of the FNO error in Figure~\ref{fig:spec_fno} has a specific and instructive cause, and isolating it shows where the physics term makes a visible difference. As Section~\ref{subsubsec:fno_time} explains, the operator applies the Fourier transform along the temporal axis as well as the spatial one, implicitly assuming the trajectory is periodic in time. It is not. The solution at $t = T$ does not match at $t = 0$, introducing jumps, so the assumption manufactures an artificial discontinuity at the temporal boundary and excites the Gibbs phenomenon there \parencite{gibbs1899}. The error curves of Figures~\ref{fig:ab_fno} and~\ref{fig:ab_pino}, drawn earlier for the parameter sweep, make the effect and its correction visible once they are read along the time axis rather than across parameters.

In the FNO panel (Figure~\ref{fig:ab_fno}) a spike appears at all three nonlinearities and is the dominant part of the FNO's late-time error. In the PINO panel (Figure~\ref{fig:ab_pino}) the predicted surfaces are visually identical, yet no such feature appears at the final time. The PDE residual and the initial-condition term penalize the temporal inconsistency that a periodic transform would otherwise tolerate. Suppressing the FNO's Gibbs artifact is a concrete benefit of the physics term in this study, although the correction is a suppression and not a cure and the ideal implementation would not take temporal transforms.

The panels share one feature: the high band is the hardest to move. Only the FNO brings it down by more than a modest fraction; elsewhere it ends within a factor of two of the magnitude of the reference, and in one case above it. In the PINN without data, the low band never falls below the reference either, so the worst of its error stays at low wavenumbers. The NTK spectrum of Figure~\ref{fig:ntk_panel} and the minimal experiment of Figure~\ref{fig:theory} predict this, now measured inside the full architectures.

The MLP panel (Figure~\ref{fig:sb_mlp}) shows that, since no physics term and no initial-condition term compete against the data fit, the low band is driven down while the high band barely moves, rising through most of the run before falling back to a little under where it started. This is the prediction of Section~\ref{subsec:spectral_bias} but seen for a higher-dimensional MLP.

Holding the architecture fixed and adding the supervised loss gives the same picture in both the PINN and the PINO families. Data pulls the low and mid bands down markedly, while the high band barely shifts. The pure-physics panels leave the low and mid bands above what the same architecture reaches with data, and the high band close to where an untrained network would leave it. Data moves the resolvable bands; the physics constraint does little.

The FNO is the one place where the wall is partly breached, reaching the lowest low- and mid-band errors in the figure and a high band several times lower than any other method's, yet still far from its own low band. Read across the six panels, Figure~\ref{fig:spectral_bias} makes the general point that the operators reduce spectral bias rather than remove it, even the plain FNO leaves a high-frequency residual, and every other method leaves more.

This picture matches the one anticipated in Chapter~\ref{chap:intro} that these methods complement the pseudospectral solver that sets the accuracy ceiling here rather than replacing it. Their value is in amortizing the parameter sweep and in the temporal regularization physics supplies, and it is smallest at the high wavenumbers, where the reference solver is unchallenged. Overall, this shows that physics-informed interpolation can be a somewhat reliable way of approximating the continuous solution, but fitting physics alone must be taken with a grain of salt.
