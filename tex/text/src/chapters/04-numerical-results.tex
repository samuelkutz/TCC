\chapter{NUMERICAL RESULTS}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter presents the numerical results. Section~\ref{sec:r_cost} reports what each model costs to train. Section~\ref{sec:r_bias} establishes the spectral-bias mechanism, carrying it from an exact toy calculation to the kernel of a real network. Section~\ref{sec:r_bands} follows the error through the frequency bands as training proceeds, Section~\ref{sec:r_spectrum} fixes a common parameter to compare the final spectra, and Section~\ref{sec:r_gibbs} isolates the temporal artifact that the physics term corrects. Two stress tests then push the operators off their training conditions (Section~\ref{sec:r_stress}), and Section~\ref{sec:r_synthesis} gathers the findings. The pseudospectral solver is the ground truth throughout, and each model leaves a characteristic failure mode.

\section{Training Cost}
\label{sec:r_cost}

Before asking how well the models perform, we ask what they cost. Table~\ref{tab:training} lists the size, wall-clock training time, and final training loss of the six trained models. Two quantities organize it. In capacity, the operators carry about $2.1$ million parameters, roughly ten times the $\sim\!200{,}000$ of the pointwise MLP and PINN. In training time, the models separate sharply: the MLP finishes in under three minutes, the plain FNO and the two PINNs in fifteen to twenty, and the PINO in well over an hour, because its physics residual is assembled on the finer $256 \times 256$ grid (Section~\ref{sec:m_pino}) at every iteration.

Accuracy is measured instead by the relative-error metrics of the following sections.}
    \label{tab:training}

    \autoriapropria
\end{table}

That hour buys generality. A single FNO or PINO covers the entire $20$-point family~\eqref{eq:param_grid} from one training, whereas each MLP and PINN is fixed to $\alpha = \beta = 3.21$ and must be retrained from scratch for any other value. Spread over the family, the operators, and the FNO in particular, are the cheaper route to a solution at a new parameter, since inference there is a single forward pass.

One caution belongs up front, because the final-loss column should not be read as a ranking. The losses do not share a scale: each objective sums a different subset of data, residual, and initial-condition terms. The PINO with data reaches a larger final loss ($8.1 \times 10^{-5}$) than the PINO without ($4.7 \times 10^{-5}$) because the extra supervised term adds its own residual to the total, and the sections below show that it is in fact the more accurate of the two. The plain FNO carries the smallest loss in the table ($3.6 \times 10^{-6}$) because its objective is a single data term with nothing added. Training loss is a poor proxy for accuracy here, and every comparison that follows rests on the held-out relative-error metrics of Section~\ref{sec:m_metrics}.

\section{Spectral Bias, From Theory to the Network}
\label{sec:r_bias}

The models share one behaviour above all others, and before comparing them we establish its mechanism. Spectral bias, derived in Section~\ref{subsec:spectral_bias}, predicts that gradient training resolves low frequencies before high ones at a rate set by the eigenvalues of the Neural Tangent Kernel. We confirm this in two steps. First we check the prediction where it can be made exact, on the toy Fourier-feature model of Section~\ref{sec:m_probe_theory}. Then we look for the same mechanism in the kernel of a real Boussinesq PINN, where the assumptions hold only approximately.

The toy model removes every approximation, so it is the right place to test the theory quantitatively. Its Jacobian is constant by construction, so the frozen-kernel dynamics hold exactly and the closed-form predictions of Section~\ref{subsec:spectral_bias} can be compared against measurement with nothing left to interpretation. Figure~\ref{fig:theory} reports the three checks.

Each panel confirms one prediction. In Figure~\ref{fig:theory_error} the amplitude of the error along each eigendirection follows the predicted $(1 - \mu\lambda_k)^n$ of~\eqref{eq:error_discrete}: the measured markers sit on the theoretical curves down to the float64 floor, and the directions with large eigenvalues collapse first while those with small eigenvalues linger, the per-eigendirection ordering of~\eqref{eq:error_evolution}. Figure~\ref{fig:theory_spectral} reads the same dynamics in Fourier space, where the measured per-mode amplitude tracks the prediction of~\eqref{eq:fourier_evolution} and the low wavenumbers resolve first, the frequency principle stated literally. Figure~\ref{fig:theory_iter} makes the agreement quantitative: plotting, for each mode, the measured iteration at which its amplitude first falls below the target precision against the closed-form estimate $n_k$ of~\eqref{eq:iteration_count} places the points on the $y = x$ diagonal, coloured by $\log_{10}\lambda_k$, with the smallest-eigenvalue modes demanding the most iterations. The theory holds to numerical precision on the model built to satisfy its assumptions.

With the prediction confirmed in the exact case, we look for the same mechanism in a real network, whose kernel drifts. Figure~\ref{fig:ntk_panel} shows the diagnostic of Section~\ref{sec:m_probe_ntk} on the Boussinesq PINN residual for three network widths.

The diagnostic supports both ingredients the theory depends on. The parameter drift is the clearest signature of the frozen-parameter regime: over the full run the network moves by about $1.5\%$ of its initial norm at width $8$, $0.4\%$ at width $128$, and $0.1\%$ at width $512$, shrinking monotonically as the network widens (Figure~\ref{fig:ntk_param}). The kernel drifts more, but in the same direction, from about $22\%$ at width $8$ to $20\%$ at width $128$ and $13\%$ at width $512$ in relative norm (Figure~\ref{fig:ntk_kernel}), so the frozen-kernel approximation~\eqref{eq:error_linear_ode} sharpens as the network widens, as the analysis assumes. The eigenvalue spectrum of the last panel supplies the driver: it spans many orders of magnitude and falls off steeply, with the narrow kernel collapsing to the numerical floor after a few dozen modes and the wider kernels decaying more gradually but still steeply. By~\eqref{eq:training_time} the time to resolve an eigendirection is inversely proportional to its eigenvalue, so this steep decay means every direction past the leading smooth ones is learned prohibitively slowly. This is the eigenvalue disparity that \textcite{wang2022ntk} identify as the source of spectral bias, and it sets the expectation for everything that follows: the network resolves the low-frequency structure readily and stalls on the high-frequency detail. The exact toy confirmed the prediction; the real kernel shows the same mechanism at work, and the band-tracking curves of the next section can be read as that mechanism operating inside the full architectures.

\section{Learning by Frequency Band}
\label{sec:r_bands}

We now watch spectral bias develop as the optimizer runs. Figure~\ref{fig:spectral_bias} tracks the relative spectral error in the low, mid, and high bands of~\eqref{eq:bands} across training for all six models. Within any single panel the figure tells one clean story; across panels it carries the caveat of Section~\ref{sec:m_metrics}, that the operators are monitored on the near-linear sample $\alpha = \beta = 0.1$ while the pointwise MLP and PINN are monitored on the harder $\alpha = \beta = 3.21$. Cross-family magnitudes are therefore not directly comparable in this figure, and the matched comparison waits for Section~\ref{sec:r_spectrum}, where every model is evaluated at the common parameter $3.21$.

The ordering within each panel is the same in all six cases: the low band is driven lowest, the mid band trails, and the high band is resolved last and least. Every architecture and every regime obeys this ordering, which is the measured face of the NTK spectrum of Figure~\ref{fig:ntk_panel} and the toy validation of Figure~\ref{fig:theory}. What varies from panel to panel is the size of the gap between the bands and how far each is pushed down.

We read the MLP panel (Figure~\ref{fig:sb_mlp}) first, because it isolates spectral bias most cleanly: with no physics term and no initial-condition term competing against the data fit, its ordering carries no confounders. The low band falls from $0.837$ at the first logged epoch to $0.015$ at the last, almost two orders of magnitude; the mid band falls from $1.004$ to $0.114$; and the high band moves only from $1.009$ to $0.704$. The low frequencies are resolved and the high frequencies are left almost untouched, the prediction of Section~\ref{subsec:spectral_bias} in its cleanest empirical form. Every other panel is a variation on it with data or physics added.

The comparisons that are valid across panels hold the architecture and the reference sample fixed and change only the data term, comparing the two regimes at the same final iteration. For the PINN at $3.21$, adding data lowers the terminal low-band error from $0.60$ to $0.06$ and the mid band from $0.82$ to $0.18$; the high band also improves, from $0.92$ to $0.70$, though it remains the worst band by far. For the PINO at $0.1$ the effect is larger in the low and mid bands: the data term drives the low band from $0.09$ to $0.02$ and the mid band from $0.27$ to $0.05$, with a smaller gain in the high band, which falls from $0.83$ to $0.58$. In both families the supervised term is a strong lever on the low and mid bands and a weak one on the high band. The physics residual alone, in the two no-data panels, never brings any band near the levels reached with data; the PINN~(no~data) high band barely moves over the whole run, ending at $0.92$, and the PINO~(no~data) high band ends at $0.83$.

The FNO panel, monitored at the same near-linear parameter as the PINO, reaches the lowest low- and mid-band errors in the figure ($0.013$ and $0.016$) and the lowest high-band error too ($0.151$). Even so, its high band sits an order of magnitude above its low band. Read across its panels, Figure~\ref{fig:spectral_bias} shows that operators compress spectral bias rather than remove it: the best model still leaves a high-frequency residual near $15\%$, and every other model leaves more.

The pointwise panels show the same failure, already present in the initial state. Both PINN regimes fail to represent the high-wavenumber content of the $\sech^2$ profile at $t = 0$, and the relative error climbs toward unity as the mode index grows. Without data (Figure~\ref{fig:spec_pinn_nodata}) the predicted spectrum has shed most of its mid- and high-frequency amplitude by $t = 30$, and the mean spectral error reaches $0.81$. Adding data (Figure~\ref{fig:spec_pinn_data}) tightens the low-wavenumber crests, in step with the low-band gain of Figure~\ref{fig:spectral_bias}, yet the aggregate mean improves only modestly ($0.17 \to 0.60 \to 0.70$), because the mid and high modes dominate it and the data term does not recover them at this hard parameter. The MLP (Figure~\ref{fig:spec_mlp}) tells the same story with the physics stripped away: it fits the reference where the frequencies are low and undershoots where they are high, its mean spectral error rising from $0.17$ at $t = 0$ to $0.53$ at $t = 15$ and $0.65$ at $t = 30$. Data helps the pointwise models where the kernel is favourable but not where it is adverse.

\section{Temporal Stability from the Physics Residual}
\label{sec:r_gibbs}

The temporal growth of the FNO error in Figure~\ref{fig:spec_fno} has a specific and instructive cause, and isolating it shows where the physics term makes the clearest difference. As Section~\ref{sec:m_fno} explains, the operator applies the Fourier transform along the temporal axis as well as the spatial one, implicitly assuming the trajectory is periodic in time. It is not: the field at $t = T$ does not match the field at $t = 0$, so the assumption manufactures an artificial discontinuity at the temporal boundary and excites the Gibbs phenomenon there \parencite{gibbs1899}. \section{Two Stress Tests}
\label{sec:r_stress}

The comparisons so far held the operators on their training conditions. Two stress tests now push them off those conditions, one along the nonlinearity axis and one along the query resolution. Both concern the operators alone, since each pointwise MLP and PINN is trained at a single parameter and a single resolution and has nothing to say about either sweep.

The first test sweeps the nonlinearity. The box plots at the foot of Figures~\ref{fig:ab_fno} and~\ref{fig:ab_pino} summarize the distribution of the time-resolved error~\eqref{eq:rel_time} over the whole trajectory at each of the three parameters, and the two operators order the parameters differently. For the FNO, the \emph{smallest} nonlinearity, $\alpha = \beta = 0.1$, carries the \emph{largest} median error, about $0.17$, against $0.12$ and $0.14$ at the more nonlinear $3.21$ and $4.2$. For the PINO with data the order reverses: the strongly nonlinear $4.2$ is the hardest, with a median near $0.08$, while $0.1$ and $3.21$ sit lower, near $0.07$ and $0.06$. Two effects plausibly combine to make the FNO's near-linear case its worst. First, $\alpha = \beta = 0.1$ sits at the extreme lower edge of the training grid~\eqref{eq:param_grid}, where the operator has the least surrounding data to interpolate from, so a boundary-of-range penalty is expected. Second, the relative metric~\eqref{eq:rel_time} divides by the instantaneous norm of the reference: in the near-linear regime the solution is dominated by the two clean, slowly deforming pulses of the wave-equation limit, whose energy is concentrated and whose norm is modest, so a fixed absolute error reads as a larger relative one. In the PINO with data, the physics residual holds the low and mid modes accurate across the range, and the residual error concentrates instead at the strongly nonlinear end, where the dynamics are genuinely hardest. The two panels agree only that the relative error is non-monotone in the parameter; which end is worst depends on the model.

The second test queries the operator off its training resolution, probing the discretization invariance operators are meant to enjoy. The box plot shows the relative error lowest at resolution $256$, with a median near $0.06$, and higher and comparable at $128$ and $512$, near $0.09$ and $0.08$. The minimum at $256$ is not a coincidence: recall from Section~\ref{sec:m_pino} that the PINO evaluates its physics residual on a $256 \times 256$ grid during training, so $256$ is the resolution its strongest constraint actually saw. Queried at $128$ the operator loses that residual-resolved detail, and queried at $512$ it meets genuine high-wavenumber content that it never learned to produce, keeping only $16$ Fourier modes per axis. The plain FNO, whose only constraint is the data at resolution $128$, does degrade monotonically over the same sweep, its median error climbing from about $0.02$ at $128$ to $0.12$ at $256$ and $0.17$ at $512$. Zero-shot superresolution is thus a geometric property, since the operator accepts and returns fields at any resolution, but not an accuracy property: fidelity is anchored to the spectral band the training constraints resolved, and it is highest where those constraints were applied.

\section{What the Experiments Show}
\label{sec:r_synthesis}

Taken together, the experiments do not identify a single best method; they map where each one succeeds and where it fails.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spectral bias is universal, and its mechanism is confirmed here.} The toy validation of Section~\ref{sec:r_bias} matches the predicted per-mode decay and iteration counts to numerical precision, the real PINN kernel shows the same eigenvalue disparity, and every architecture then obeys the same ordering: low wavenumbers first and best, high wavenumbers last and least (Figures~\ref{fig:ntk_panel}, \ref{fig:theory}, \ref{fig:spectral_bias}). The data-only MLP is the clearest instance, driving its low band by nearly two orders of magnitude while its high band barely moves. No method drives the high band below roughly $0.15$ relative error, and the best there is the plain FNO.

    \item \textbf{Data is the decisive lever, and physics does not substitute for it.} Within both the PINN and the PINO families, adding the supervised term sharply cuts the low- and mid-band error, while the physics-only regimes stay close to the biased baseline (Figures~\ref{fig:spectral_bias}, \ref{fig:spec_pino_data}, \ref{fig:spec_pino_nodata}). Physics alone neither matches data nor removes the bias.

    \item \textbf{Operators dominate at low and mid wavenumbers.} At the common parameter $3.21$ the FNO and the PINO with data track the low and mid spectrum well below either PINN or the MLP (Section~\ref{sec:r_spectrum}), and they cover the whole family from one training, whereas each pointwise model is bound to a single parameter.

    \item \textbf{Physics buys temporal stability.} The FNO's error grows in time and spikes at the temporal boundary through the Gibbs artifact of its periodic time axis, and the PINO's residual suppresses the spike and keeps the late-time error bounded (Section~\ref{sec:r_gibbs}). This is the clearest concrete benefit of the physics term in the study.

    \item \textbf{The remaining limitations are real.} The relative error is non-monotone in nonlinearity, with the hardest parameter differing between the two operators (Section~\ref{sec:r_stress}); zero-shot accuracy is best at the resolution the physics residual saw and worse away from it, rather than invariant (Section~\ref{sec:r_stress}); and the high-frequency band is never fully resolved by any method. These are consistent features of the design, not artefacts of a single run.
\end{itemize}

The picture matches the one anticipated in Chapter~\ref{chap:intro}: these methods complement the pseudospectral solver that sets the accuracy ceiling here rather than replacing it. Their value is in amortizing the cost of the parameter sweep and in the temporal regularization that physics supplies, and it is least of all at the high wavenumbers where the reference solver stays unchallenged.
