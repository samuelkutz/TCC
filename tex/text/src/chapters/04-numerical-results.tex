\chapter{NUMERICAL RESULTS}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter reports what the experiments of Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} produced. Section~\ref{sec:r_cost} accounts for what each method costs to train. Section~\ref{sec:r_nn_comparison} puts the MLP and both PINN regimes side by side at their trained parameter, and Section~\ref{sec:r_no_comparison} puts the FNO and the PINO side by side, sweeping the nonlinearity parameter at a fixed resolution (Section~\ref{sec:r_params}) and then querying both operators off their training resolution (Section~\ref{sec:r_res}). Section~\ref{sec:r_spectrum} fixes both and compares the spectra of all six methods mode by mode at three time instants. Section~\ref{sec:r_bias} tests the spectral-bias theory of Chapter~\ref{chap:math-bg} on a minimal network, and Section~\ref{sec:r_bands} follows the same bias through training in the six trained methods. The pseudospectral solver is the ground truth throughout.

\section{Training Cost}
\label{sec:r_cost}

Before asking how well the methods perform, we need to ask what they cost to train. Table~\ref{tab:training} lists the size, training time, and final training loss of the six trained methods. It is important to notice that neural operator methods carry many more trainable parameters than the neural network methods. Specifically, PINO is slower because it assembles its physics residual on the finer $256 \times 256$ grid (Table~\ref{tab:m_config}) at every iteration.

\section{Spectral Comparison of the Six Methods}
\label{sec:r_spectrum}

To compare the families on equal terms we fix $\alpha = \beta = 3{,}21$ and inspect the full spatial spectrum of the prediction at three times, $t = 0$, $t = T/2 = 15$, and $t = T = 30$. Figures~\ref{fig:spec_mlp}--\ref{fig:spec_pino_nodata} report the six methods. In every panel the black curve is the reference amplitude $|\hat\eta_{\text{true}}(k,t_n)|$ and the dashed orange curve is the prediction $|\hat\eta_{\text{pred}}(k,t_n)|$, both read off the DFT~\eqref{eq:dft}. 

Beside each spectrum is also the per-mode absolute error $E_{\text{spec}}(k,t_n)$ of~\eqref{eq:m_abs_spec}, and at the foot of each figure is that same error averaged over every mode, $\widetilde{E}_{\text{spec}}(t_n)$ of~\eqref{eq:m_spec_mean}. One reading note applies throughout. The per-mode error~\eqref{eq:m_abs_spec} is a difference of amplitudes in the units of $\eta$, not a ratio, so it is read against the reference amplitude at the same wavenumber.

\section{Benchmark for Spectral Bias MLP}
\label{sec:r_bias}

Here, we aim to take a turn into investigating the spectral bias of basic MLPs. Derived in Section~\ref{subsec:spectral_bias}, this effect predicts that gradient training resolves low frequencies before high ones at a rate set by the eigenvalues of the Neural Tangent Kernel. Our experiment of Section~\ref{sec:m_minimal} puts that prediction on the Boussinesq system in its barest form with the Spectral Bias MLP learning the single profile $\eta(x, 15)$ at $\alpha = \beta = 3{,}21$, at three widths, by just gradient descent at the per-width step $\mu = 0{,}4/\lambda_{\max}$, the largest initial NTK eigenvalue, run for half a million iterations. As one can see, the behavior of the low band is similar across all methods, with the error dropping quickly and leveling off. The mid band shows a more gradual decrease, while the high band remains relatively flat, indicating that these methods struggle to capture high-frequency components effectively. The choice of normalization technique here mattered empirically for us; we saw that the low band fall off was much faster and consistent across all methods without our normalization~\eqref{eq:m_minmax}. After implementation of our presented normalization, the low band fall off was less consistent and clear.

This picture matches the one anticipated in Chapter~\ref{chap:intro} that these methods complement the pseudospectral solver that sets the accuracy ceiling here rather than replacing it. Their value is in amortizing the parameter sweep and in the temporal regularization physics supplies, and it is smallest at the high wavenumbers, where the reference solver is unchallenged. Overall, this shows that physics-informed interpolation can be a somewhat reliable way of approximating the continuous solution, but fitting physics alone must be taken with a grain of salt.
